# Editor's review: "Worthy Numbers" (11 pages)

Overall: the book looks clean and reads fast. The arithmetic holds almost everywhere. The two biggest problems are logic, not typos. Page 5 dismisses a "share of energy" just before page 8 makes a share of energy the hero number. Page 8 also presents an example target as "your target". The house rules hold on dashes, banned words and -ly words. Symmetric pairs are the main rule break, with seven of them.

Findings are ranked by how much they matter to a cold reader.

---

## Findings

### 1. Page 5 contradicts page 8 (share of energy vs PD)
- **Current (p5):** "A share says nothing about your portion. The grams of protein in what you eat do." / Use it: "Read the claim as a first filter. Then count the grams of protein in your portion."
- **Problem:** PD is the same ratio as the claim (PD × 4 = % of energy from protein), so page 5 tells the reader to count grams and page 8 tells them to trust a ratio. The second sentence is also a symmetric pair, and its trailing "do" leaves the reader to work out the referent.
- **Replacement (body):** "The claim is a yes or no at one line, and it rates the pack on its own. 20% of energy from protein is the same as PD 5: 5 g of protein per 100 kcal. Page 8 uses that ratio for your whole meal."
- **Replacement (Use it):** "Use the claim to shortlist a product. Then check the PD of the meal you build with it."
- Option: move this page to sit right before PD (page 7), so the share leads straight into the ratio.

### 2. Page 3: the "multiply" instruction gives the wrong answer
- **Current:** "At the shelf, compare per 100 g. At the table, multiply by your grams."
- **Problem:** Read literally, 450 kcal × 60 = 27,000 kcal. The reader also has to divide by 100. The line is a mirrored pair as well.
- **Replacement:** "Compare packs per 100 g. For your bowl, multiply by your grams and divide by 100: 450 kcal × 60 ÷ 100 = 270 kcal."

### 3. Page 8: an example target is labelled "your target"
- **Current:** "Your target comes from your day. 180 g of protein on 2,800 kcal is PD 6.4." Chart: "your target 6.4" and "target 6.4".
- **Problem:** 180 g on 2,800 kcal fits a large person who trains. A cold reader takes 6.4 as their own number, and page 9 then grades the bowl against it. The book never says how to work out your own target. The chart also shows the target label twice.
- **Replacement:** "Your target comes from your day: your protein grams divided by your kcal, times 100. One example: 180 g of protein on 2,800 kcal is PD 6.4. This book uses 6.4 as its example target." Chart: change to "example target 6.4" and drop the duplicate top-right label.

### 4. Page 8: no pack prints PD, and the page doesn't say so
- **Current:** "PD is grams of protein per 100 kcal. It is a ratio, so it works the same way for a food, a meal or a whole day."
- **Problem:** Next to EU-regulated numbers, a cold reader assumes PD is printed on packs too, and looks for it. The page also never shows the calculation once.
- **Replacement (add after the first sentence):** "No pack prints PD. You work it out from two lines of the label, protein and energy. Granola: 9 g ÷ 450 kcal × 100 = PD 2."

### 5. Page 2: "Six numbers on your food" isn't true, and the subline miscounts
- **Current:** "Six numbers on your food" / "One number on each page: what it tells you, and how to use it at your next meal."
- **Problem:** Only three appear on packs (per 100 g, Nutri-Score, the protein claim). Page 6 itself says "You won't find these scores on a pack", GI and GL are rarely printed, and PD is never printed. Pages 5, 6 and 7 each carry two numbers.
- **Replacement:** Title "Six numbers about your food". Subline: "The pack prints three of them: per 100 g, Nutri-Score and the protein claim. The other three you look up or work out, and each page shows how."

### 6. Page 9: no verdict on 6.3 vs 6.4, and the closer is an aphorism pair
- **Current:** "From 3.4 to 6.3, against a target of 6.4" / Use it: "The pack tells you about the food. Your grams and your day tell you about the meal."
- **Problem:** The bowl still misses the target, and the reader is never told whether that matters. The Use it line is a mirrored pair and an aphorism closer.
- **Replacement (Use it):** "6.3 is close enough to a 6.4 target to eat as it is. When a bowl sits well under your target, add one protein food, as the skyr did here." (Author: set your own tolerance if "close enough" needs a number.)

### 7. Page 11: the back-cover bowl doesn't match the book's bowl
- **Current:** "Your breakfast / 30 g granola with 200 ml milk 1.5% PD 4.1 / Chef Mealan adds 80 g of skyr PD 6.5"
- **Problem:** The reader has just followed a 60 g bowl (PD 3.4). Here the granola has halved with no explanation, so the app's change is hidden. The arithmetic is right: 229 kcal and 9.5 g gives 4.1, and 279 kcal and 18.3 g gives 6.5.
- **Replacement:** Row 1 "The bowl from page 9: 60 g granola with 200 ml milk 1.5%, PD 3.4". Row 2 "Chef Mealan: 30 g granola, 200 ml milk 1.5% and 80 g skyr, PD 6.5". This version shows the app hitting the target on 279 kcal rather than 459. A simpler alternative keeps 60 g and adds 160 g of skyr for PD 6.4 (465 kcal, 29.8 g).

### 8. The book never answers its own cover promise
- **Current (cover):** "The numbers on your food, and which ones help you decide."
- **Problem:** No page says which numbers decide a meal. The reader has to infer that PD is the hero from the red row on the contents page.
- **Replacement (add as the intro line on p9, under the heading):** "Two numbers decided this bowl: the grams you poured and its PD. The other four helped pick the products."

### 9. Page 3 headline: "about" reads as "approximately"
- **Current:** "The label is about 100 g. You eat a portion."
- **Problem:** A cold reader parses "about 100 g" as "roughly 100 g". The line is also a mirrored pair.
- **Replacement:** "The label counts per 100 g, not per bowl."

### 10. Page 9 "Claim" row: unclear which bowl, and no verdict
- **Current:** "25% of the bowl's energy is protein"
- **Problem:** The 25% is true only for the skyr bowl (114.8 of 458.5 kcal). The poured bowl is 13%. The row also doesn't say what the claim rule would conclude.
- **Replacement:** "With skyr, 25% of the energy is protein, above the 20% High protein line. As poured, 13%."

### 11. Page 3: "Every pack" overclaims, and the sample label leaves out kJ
- **Current:** "Every pack in the EU lists the same values per 100 g or 100 ml: energy, fat and saturates, carbohydrate and sugars, protein and salt." / "Per 100 g is made for comparing two packs on the shelf."
- **Problem:** Regulation 1169/2011 Annex V exempts single-ingredient unprocessed foods, coffee, tea, spices, water and very small packs. EU labels must show energy in kJ and kcal, and the reader's pack will lead with kJ. "Is made for" is passive.
- **Replacement:** "Almost every EU pack lists the same seven values per 100 g or 100 ml: energy in kJ and kcal, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. Plain foods like coffee, tea and spices can skip it." / "Use per 100 g to compare two packs on the shelf." In the table, change the energy row to "1,883 kJ / 450 kcal" and "1,130 kJ / 270 kcal".

### 12. Page 4: Nutri-Score is voluntary, and the page doesn't say so
- **Current:** "It started in France in 2017 and is recommended in Belgium, Germany, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Spain and Switzerland. An updated formula has applied since 2024 in most of these countries."
- **Problem:** The countries and dates check out. Germany, Belgium, Switzerland and the Netherlands moved to the new formula on 1 Jan 2024, Luxembourg in March 2024 and France in March 2025. But the label is voluntary: only about 41% of French products showed it in 2026, and brands keep dropping it (Nestlé in Switzerland by end 2026). The reader needs to know a missing letter is normal and that letters changed. "Is recommended" is passive.
- **Replacement:** "France started it in 2017. Belgium, Germany, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Spain and Switzerland back it too. It is voluntary, so many packs carry no letter. The formula changed in 2024 and 2025, so an older pack can show a different letter."

### 13. Page 7: GI does not measure "how fast", and the headline is a mirrored pair
- **Current:** Headline "GI says how fast. GL adds how much." / "GI ranks carbohydrate foods from 0 to 100 by how fast they raise blood glucose in the two hours after eating. It is measured on a fixed amount of carbohydrate, not on your portion."
- **Problem:** GI measures the total rise over two hours from 50 g of carbohydrate, compared with pure glucose at 100. Speed is a loose shorthand. The reader is never told what 100 means. "It is measured" is passive.
- **Replacement:** Headline "GL is GI with your portion added." Body: "GI rates how much 50 g of carbohydrate from a food raises blood glucose over two hours, against pure glucose at 100. Labs test it on that fixed amount, never on your portion." Contents row: change "how fast, how much" to "0 to 100, per portion".

### 14. Page 7: no route to a GI value
- **Current:** "Use GI to compare two carbohydrate foods."
- **Problem:** EU packs rarely print GI, so the reader can't act on the advice.
- **Replacement:** "Packs rarely print GI. Look it up in the University of Sydney GI database at glycemicindex.com, then compare two carbohydrate foods."

### 15. Page 6: no example scores, an embedded question and passives
- **Current:** "A quality score asks two things: does the protein bring enough of each, and how much of it does your body take up?" / "DIAAS, recommended by the FAO in 2013, measures each amino acid where it is absorbed, and has no cap."
- **Problem:** The page gives no values, so the bars carry no information. The question form and the passive "where it is absorbed" both break house rules. The facts check out: PDCAAS truncates at 1.0, the FAO proposed DIAAS in 2013, and DIAAS is untruncated.
- **Replacement:** "A quality score checks two things: whether the protein brings enough of each, and how much of it your body takes up." / "DIAAS, which the FAO recommended in 2013, measures each amino acid at the end of the small intestine, where your body absorbs it. It has no cap. On DIAAS, milk protein scores about 1.2, soy about 0.9 and wheat under 0.5." Then mark those three on the DIAAS bar.

### 16. Page 5: the example bar misses the 20% line
- **Current:** "Protein bar, 60 g / 250 kcal / 20%: High protein / about 12 g protein"
- **Problem:** 12 g × 4 kcal = 48 kcal, which is 19.2% of 250, so that bar could not carry the claim. It needs at least 12.5 g.
- **Replacement:** "about 13 g protein" (52 kcal, 21%).

### 17. Page 9: the label and GI rows restate facts instead of using them
- **Current:** "The label: 9 g protein per 100 g of granola" / "GI and GL: Most carbohydrate comes from the granola"
- **Problem:** The page promises "every number" but these rows don't apply their number to this bowl.
- **Replacement:** "60 g of granola gives 5.4 g protein, from 9 g per 100 g" / "With skyr, about 52 g carbohydrate, 36 g of it from the granola. Less granola lowers the GL most." (This assumes milk at 4.8 g and skyr at 4 g carbohydrate per 100.)

### 18. Page 2: the contents "what it says" column is cryptic
- **Current:** "Protein quality 1.0", "PD, for your meal 6.3", and no row for page 10.
- **Problem:** "1.0" and "6.3" mean nothing to someone who hasn't read the book yet. "PD" isn't expanded, and 6.3 clashes with the 6.4 on page 8.
- **Replacement:** "Protein quality: score up to 1.0", "Protein density (PD): protein per 100 kcal", and add "About the author 10".

### 19. Page 8: mirrored pair, a metaphor and an unclear referent
- **Current:** "A plate at your target keeps the day on track. A plate under it leaves the gap for later meals."
- **Problem:** The two sentences mirror each other, "on track" is a metaphor nothing uses, and "the gap" has no referent.
- **Replacement:** "When a plate comes in under your target, your later meals have to make up the missing protein."

### 20. Page 4 "Use it" is a mirrored pair
- **Current:** "Compare with the letter. Decide with your portion and your plate."
- **Problem:** The two halves mirror each other and read as a slogan.
- **Replacement:** "Use the letter to pick between two similar products, then judge the meal by your portion and what else is on the plate."

### 21. Page 10: the author page claims recipes and gives no nutrition credential
- **Current:** "I try every recipe and every number in this book on myself first."
- **Problem:** The book has no recipes. The page also gives the reader no reason to trust the nutrition content.
- **Replacement:** "I test every number in this book on my own meals first. I'm also a certified fitness and nutrition coach [name the certificate]."

### 22. Page 10: recruiters get vague claims
- **Current:** "I have spent about 20 years building software products as a product manager, and I founded several startups along the way." / "I design and build Chef Mealan with AI, from the first sketch to every release, so these numbers work at every meal without a calculator in your hand."
- **Problem:** "About" and "several" weaken the record. The second sentence runs two ideas together.
- **Replacement:** "I have spent 20 years building B2B and B2B2C SaaS products as a product manager, and I founded [number] startups along the way." / "I design and build Chef Mealan with AI, from the first sketch to every release [one concrete detail, such as release count or evals]. The app does the arithmetic in this book at every meal."

### 23. Page 11: the code and the craving bullet are unclear
- **Current:** "Leave your name and email, and you get a personal code to join." / "A craving answered with the food that makes it fit your day."
- **Problem:** The page never says what the code lets you join, and "a craving answered" is hard to parse.
- **Replacement:** "...a personal code to join [the Chef Mealan beta / early access]." / "Tell it what you crave, and it suggests a version that fits your day."

### 24. Page 11: the reader can't tell how this book fits the series
- **Current:** "The Worth series: Worth Building is out now, and Worth Eating comes next."
- **Problem:** The page doesn't say whether "Worthy Numbers" belongs to the Worth series. Worth Building is a product book, which a food reader won't expect.
- **Replacement:** "Worthy Numbers opens the Worth series on food. Worth Eating comes next. Worth Building, on product thinking, is out now." (Adjust to the real series structure.)

## Rule-break index (each line quoted above)

- **Symmetric pairs:** p3 headline (#9), p3 Use it (#2), p4 Use it (#20), p5 "A share says nothing..." (#1), p7 headline (#13), p8 plate pair (#19), p9 Use it (#6). The p4 "It helps / It can't" boxes are a layout device and acceptable.
- **Passive voice:** p3 "is made for" (#11), p4 "is recommended in" (#12), p6 "where it is absorbed" (#15), p7 "It is measured" (#13).
- **Question form:** p6 (#15). There are no rhetorical questions.
- **Aphorism closers:** p9 Use it (#6). p4 Use it comes close (#20).
- **Unused metaphors:** p8 "on track" (#19). "Read the claim as a first filter" goes with #1.
- **Unclear referents:** p5 "do" (#1), p8 "the gap" (#19), p9 "the bowl's" (#10), p2 "1.0" and "6.3" (#18).
- **Clean:** no em or en dashes, none of the three banned words, no -ly intensifiers, and "Chef Mealan" is spelled the same way everywhere.

## Numbers checked and correct

- p3: all the 60 g values are correct (salt 0.18 rounds to 0.2). Fat, carbohydrate and protein give 438 kcal, and fibre explains the rest of the 450.
- p5: EU 1924/2006 thresholds (12% and 20% of energy) are correct. Skyr 200 g gives 126 kcal and 22 g protein, so 70% is correct, and the bar positions match.
- p7: GL low at 10 or less and high at 20 or more is correct. 60 × 15 ÷ 100 = 9 and 60 × 30 ÷ 100 = 18, and the bars match.
- p8: 180 ÷ 2,800 × 100 = 6.4, granola 2.0, skyr 17.5 (rounds to 18), and the dots sit at the right positions. "Chicken and rice about 8" works for a plate with oil and a generous amount of rice. Consider naming the grams.
- p9: 364 kcal and 12.2 g give 3.4. 458.5 kcal and 28.7 g give 6.3.
- p11: 229 kcal and 9.5 g give 4.1. 279 kcal and 18.3 g give 6.5.
- Nutri-Score: 2017 is correct, and the six countries match Santé publique France. PDCAAS truncates at 1.0. DIAAS was recommended in 2013.

## The three changes I would make first

1. **Reconcile pages 5 and 8 (#1, #4).** Say that "High protein" at 20% equals PD 5, that no pack prints PD, and show the granola calculation once.
2. **Turn "your target 6.4" into an example target and give the method (#3), then give a verdict on 6.3 against 6.4 on page 9 (#6).**
3. **Fix the page 3 arithmetic instruction (#2), and retitle the contents to "Six numbers about your food" with an honest subline (#5).**
