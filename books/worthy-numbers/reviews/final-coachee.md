# Worthy Numbers: feedback from a reader

Who I am: mid-thirties, office job, gym two or three times a week. I want to lose a bit of fat and get stronger. I do look at labels in the supermarket, but mostly I look at calories and sugar and then put the thing in the trolley anyway. No nutrition training. I clicked a social post, got the PDF, read it on my phone first and then again on a laptop. Took me about ten minutes the first time.

---

## Page by page

### Page 1, cover

Looks professional. Big title, clean. "A guide to food labels" told me what it is, which is good, because "Worthy Numbers" on its own tells me nothing.

The dot bowl took me a second, then I saw it was a bowl. Nice.

"Product and recipe builder, and the maker of Chef Mealan." I don't know what a recipe builder is. I don't know what Chef Mealan is. I guessed it's a pun on meal and Milan. So the first thing under the author's name is two things I've never heard of.

### Page 2, contents

I skimmed it. The middle column confused me a bit:
- "Protein quality: 1.0". 1.0 of what?
- "GI and GL: 0 to 100, per portion". No idea what that means yet.

The red line for "Protein density (PD)" made it obvious that page 8 is the main thing. Good, I knew where the book was heading.

"Six numbers", but I counted the rows and got more than six, then worked out the last two aren't numbers. Small thing.

### Page 3, the label

**This is where I got interested.** "The portion is the maker's guess" is something I never thought about. And "Your bowl is a different number: what you pour, plus what you pour over it." Yes. I pour granola straight from the bag and have no idea how much.

The table with "Per 100 g" next to "Your 60 g" is really clear. The sum at the bottom (450 × 60 ÷ 100 = 270) I could actually follow.

Not understood:
- "kJ". I see this on every pack. I assumed it's another kind of calorie. Still don't know if I should care.
- "saturates". I guessed saturated fat, the bad kind. Not sure.
- "kcal". I call them calories. I think they're the same. The book never says so.

### Page 4, Nutri-Score

Quick and useful. I've seen these letters on packs and never knew what they were based on. "It can't tell you whether your lunch fits your day" made sense.

I skimmed the history bit (France 2017, list of countries). "An updated formula has applied since 2024": does that mean a pack with a B last year might be a C now? It raises a question and then leaves it.

### Page 5, High protein

**Second place I really woke up.** I buy "high protein" stuff. I thought it meant a lot of protein. The protein bar showing "High protein" but only about 13 g, next to the skyr with about 22 g for half the calories. That was a proper surprise. That one picture will stick with me.

"A share of the energy, not an amount." I had to read this twice. I didn't connect "energy" with calories at first. Once I did, it made sense: of all the calories in this thing, 20% come from protein.

The little black ticks on the bars: I didn't get they were the 12% and 20% lines until I saw the small grey text at the top right.

"Skyr": I know it, it's that thick yoghurt. Someone who doesn't shop in Germany or Scandinavia might not.

### Page 6, protein quality

**This is where I lost interest.** PDCAAS, DIAAS, FAO, amino acids, "the end of the small intestine". It felt like a school textbook. I read the headline and the blue box and skipped the middle.

Not understood:
- "PDCAAS" and "DIAAS". Just letters to me. I gave up trying to read them.
- "FAO". Some official food body, I guessed.
- "amino acids... some of them only food can give you". OK, I sort of knew this from the gym.
- "It caps every protein at 1.0, so the best ones look the same." So what? I didn't see why I should care which score is newer.
- Wheat 0.4 on the chart. Does that mean the protein in my bread is less than half as good? Should I count it less? The page doesn't say.

What I kept: meat, dairy and soy are fine. If I eat plant protein, mix it, like beans with rice. That's one line. The rest of the page is there to get to that line.

### Page 7, GI and GL

**I skimmed most of this.** I'm not diabetic and nobody has ever told me to worry about blood sugar. The page never says why someone like me, who wants to lose fat, should care about GI.

Not understood:
- "blood glucose". Blood sugar, I assume.
- "against pure glucose at 100". Lost me.
- "medium" on the 30 g example. The text only defines low (10 or less) and high (20 or more). I worked out medium is the gap in between, but it's not said.
- Looking it up at glycemicindex.com. I'm not going to do that in a supermarket aisle.

The formula was easy enough to follow with the example, I'll give it that. But I didn't take anything away I'd use.

### Page 8, PD

**This is the page I came for, and it mostly worked.** "Grams of protein per 100 kcal" and then the granola worked out (9 ÷ 450 × 100 = 2). I could follow that. I tried it on my own protein bar straight away with my phone calculator.

"PD is the protein claim from page 5 in another unit. Source of protein is PD 3, and High protein is PD 5." I believed it, but I couldn't see why 20% equals 5. I only got it later when I remembered from somewhere that a gram of protein has about 4 calories. The book doesn't tell you that, so the link between page 5 and page 8 is something you have to take on trust.

Then: "Your own target comes from your goal, your body and your training." **This is where I got stuck.** The only target I'm given is 6.4, for someone eating 2,350 calories and 150 g of protein. I don't know my calories. I don't know how much protein I need. So I can work out a PD, but I have nothing to compare it to except someone else's number.

"One example, worked out by Chef Mealan: 150 g of protein on 2,350 kcal is PD 6.4." It's just 150 divided by 2,350. I can do that myself. Crediting an app for a division felt a bit odd, like the advert started in the middle of the lesson.

"Rice weighed cooked" on the chart. I didn't know why that was there. I figured out later it's because dry rice and cooked rice weigh very different amounts. Nobody explained that, and it matters if I weigh my own rice.

The chart was a nice surprise: chicken and rice comes out at about 10, way above the target. I thought rice would drag it down. So that was good news.

The blue box: "add or swap one food with higher PD and calculate its PD again". Whose PD, the food's or the plate's? I think the plate's. Took me a second.

### Page 9, one bowl, every number

**This is the page that made it click.** Same granola. Swap the milk for skyr. Calories go from 364 to 377, basically the same. Protein doubles. PD goes from 3.4 to 6.4. That's a real swap I can do tomorrow morning. Simple and convincing.

The table underneath I skimmed. It's a recap of the earlier pages, and some of it doesn't really fit a bowl. "Claim: 26% of the energy is protein with skyr." My bowl doesn't have a claim on it.

What's missing: how did you get 364 and 12.2? I assume you added the granola numbers to the milk numbers. The book never shows that. And my first instinct was to average the two PDs (granola 2, skyr 17, so about 9.5). That's wrong, the real answer is 6.4. If I'd done this on my own I'd have got it wrong and not known.

"Aim near your target at each meal." Every meal? What about an apple, or a coffee? An apple has almost no protein. Is a piece of fruit a "bad" meal now? This line made me a bit nervous about snacks.

### Page 10, about the author

The photo is just a grey box. I want to see who's telling me what to eat.

"B2B and B2B2C software products", "agentic era". I don't know what these mean and they don't matter to me. I skimmed the first paragraph.

What matters to me is "certified fitness and nutrition coach" and "I cook every day for myself and my family, and I test every number in this book on my own meals". Those are the lines that make me trust him. They're buried in the middle and at the end. The startup and the event planning app came first, and I don't care about either.

### Page 11, back cover

"Live the day, so it counts." Sounds like a gym poster. Didn't do anything for me.

The paragraph is a long list: calories in and out, protein, carbs, fat, vitamins, minerals, activity, training, sleep, recovery. It reads like everything. I glazed over.

The example box is the same 80 kg man keeping his weight. I'm not that person. I want to lose fat. So I'm seeing the same number for the third time and it still isn't mine.

The three bullets are the most interesting part of the page. "Name a craving, and Chef Mealan finds the food that makes it fit your day" made me smile. That's the one feature I'd actually want. The weekly plan and the shopping list are useful too.

"Spots are limited." Felt like a sales trick.

"Worth Building is available, and Worth Eating comes soon." No idea what these are. Not for me.

## Overall

### 1. Where I was interested, where I skimmed

Interested: page 3 (the portion is the maker's guess), page 5 (the protein bar vs the skyr), page 8 (the PD sum), page 9 (the skyr swap). Those four pages are the book for me.

Skimmed: page 6 almost completely, page 7 mostly, the first paragraph of page 10, the long list on page 11.

It's short, which I liked. It looks good. The "Use it" boxes are the best idea in the book. On the pages I skimmed, I still read the blue box.

### 3. What I'll actually do differently

At the next shop:
- Turn over anything that says "High protein" and look at the grams for the amount I'd actually eat. Not the claim.
- Work out the PD of my usual protein bar and compare it to skyr or quark. I already did this for one bar and it came out lower than I expected.
- Use the Nutri-Score letter to choose between two granolas. I never used it before.

At the next meal:
- Weigh my granola once to see what I really pour. I suspect it's a lot more than 60 g.
- Try granola with skyr instead of milk.

What I won't do: look up GI, think about DIAAS, or calculate the PD of a full dinner. That last one is too much work without knowing my target.

### 4. Can I work out a PD and use it?

In my own words: PD is how many grams of protein you get for every 100 calories. You take the protein number off the label, divide by the calories, and times by 100. Higher means more protein for the same calories. Granola is about 2. Skyr is about 17.

**Could I work it out?** Yes, for one product with a label. Easy, with my phone calculator.

**Could I use it?** Only halfway. Two problems:

1. **I don't know my own target.** The only target in the book is 6.4, for an 80 kg man keeping his weight. I want to lose fat. I don't know if my number is higher or lower, and the book says the app works it out, not me.
2. **I don't know how to do a whole plate.** The book shows the totals on page 9 but not how to get them. My instinct was to average the PDs, which turns out to be wrong. You have to add up all the protein, add up all the calories, then divide. I only figured that out by checking the numbers myself.

Where it clicked: page 9, the skyr swap. Same calories, double the protein. That's when "higher PD" stopped being a number and became something I can do.

Where it didn't: the moment the book said "your own target" and then didn't give me one.

### 5. Would I scan the code?

Maybe. About 50/50.

For it: the book got me interested in my own target, then said the app is where I get it. That works on me a bit. Free is good. The craving feature sounds fun, and a shopping list would save me time.

Against:
- I have to give my email and wait up to a day. By tomorrow I'll have forgotten.
- "Free while in beta" means it costs money later. How much? Not said.
- "Spots are limited" feels like pressure.
- I don't know what it is. An app? A website? A chat? There's no picture of it anywhere in the book.
- No face on the author page. I'd be giving my weight and goals to someone I can't see.

What would make me do it:
- See one screenshot of what I'd get. Even the target screen.
- Know what it costs after beta, even roughly.
- Get something straight away, like my target number on the page after I scan, instead of waiting a day.
- An example person who looks like me: someone who wants to lose fat, not someone keeping their weight.

### 6. Preachy, salesy, too technical, not for me

- **Too technical:** page 6 (protein scores) and most of page 7 (GI and GL). Not preachy, more like school. They broke the flow between page 5 and page 8, which are the two pages that belong together.
- **Salesy:** the app comes in on page 8, in the middle of the lesson, and then appears on every page after. Page 8's "worked out by Chef Mealan" for a simple division felt forced. "Spots are limited" on the back felt pushy. The first half of the book felt like it was for me. The second half started feeling like it was for the app.
- **Not for me:** the author page's business background, and the line about the other books in the series.
- **Not preachy.** No one told me I eat badly. I liked that. "Typical values. Check your own pack." also felt honest.

### 7. One thing I wish the book had told me

**How to get a rough target of my own without the app.** Even a simple rule, something like "protein per kilo of your body weight, divided by roughly the calories you eat". With that I could use PD tomorrow. Without it, I have a sum I can do and no idea what the answer should be.
